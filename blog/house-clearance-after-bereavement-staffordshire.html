House clearance after bereavement — a Staffordshire family guide

Clearing a parent or grandparent's house after a bereavement is one of the hardest jobs a family takes on, layered with grief, sibling negotiation, paperwork, and the sheer physical scale of 40 or 50 years of accumulated possessions. We've handled around 250 bereavement clearances across Staffordshire since 2010 — from compact retirement bungalows in Trentham to four-bedroom family homes in Eccleshall — and the families we work with are almost always grateful for an outside team that handles the practical work with respect and minimal additional stress. This guide explains what bereavement clearance actually involves, the realistic timeline, what it costs in 2026, and how we approach the parts of the job that aren't really about furniture at all.

Section 1

What house clearance actually means (and how it differs from a removal)

House clearance is a different kind of operation from a removal, even though both involve moving belongings out of a property. A removal takes the contents of a home to a new home, where the customer carries on living. A clearance takes the contents of a home that won't be lived in again — usually after a bereavement, sometimes after a divorce or downsizing — and disposes of them through some combination of family redistribution, sale, donation, recycling and skip disposal.

The skill of a good bereavement clearance company is in the sorting, not the carrying. Anyone can shift a wardrobe into a skip. Knowing that the apparently-empty drawer at the back of the wardrobe contains a 1940s wedding photograph that the family will want, or that the slightly-tarnished candlestick on the mantelpiece is sterling silver worth keeping, is what distinguishes a respectful clearance from a brutal one. We train every member of our clearance crew to slow down, check before disposing, and ask before assuming. The job takes longer and costs slightly more than a brute-force clearance — that's the trade-off.

What we do during a clearance: sort the contents room-by-room into the categories the family has agreed (keep / sell / donate / recycle / dispose), photograph items the family wants to see before deciding, distribute keep-items to wherever the family wants them sent, arrange auction or dealer collection for sellable items, run loads to charity shops and recycling centres, and arrange skip disposal for anything else. Our house clearance service page has the operational detail.

What we don't do: we don't act as auctioneers or valuers (we work with established Staffordshire auctioneers and valuers for that part); we don't deal with estate-administration paperwork (that's the executor's job); and we don't make decisions about what's sentimental — those are always family decisions and we ask before disposing of anything ambiguous.

Section 2

The emotional and legal context of bereavement clearance

Bereavement clearances happen against a backdrop of grief, family dynamics, and legal estate-administration constraints. The practical work we do is straightforward — what makes the job different from a normal house clearance is the surrounding context.

Grief. Most family members coming to a bereavement clearance are still in active grief, often within weeks or a few months of the death. The familiar surroundings of the deceased's home are themselves grief-triggering, and the act of dismantling those surroundings can feel disloyal. We don't pretend to understand the specific grief of any particular family, but we've sat with enough of them to know that giving the family physical and emotional space during the clearance helps. Our crews stay out of the family's way unless we're being asked specifically about an item, we work at a calm pace rather than rushing, and we don't make small-talk about the deceased unless the family opens that conversation themselves.

Family dynamics. Siblings who haven't lived together for 30 years often find themselves negotiating over who keeps the dining table, who has the right to the family photographs, and whether the second cousin should be invited to choose any keepsakes. We stay out of these discussions entirely — they're the family's to navigate. What we ask for is a single point of contact (usually the executor or one nominated sibling) who tells us the family's decisions, and we work to those instructions without questioning them.

Probate constraints. The estate's contents are technically part of the inheritance and can't be fully cleared until probate has been granted, which typically takes 6–14 weeks in straightforward cases (longer if the estate is complex or contested). For high-value items in particular, the executor needs to keep a record of disposal so the estate accounting is complete. We provide a clearance schedule listing every significant item disposed of, with photographs where requested, to support the executor's records.

Will-specified items. If the will specifically bequeaths particular items to particular people (a great-grandfather's pocket watch to the eldest grandson, the rosewood writing desk to the nephew in Australia), those items are identified by the executor at the start of the clearance and handled separately. We tag them, hold them safely at our depot if needed, and arrange delivery to the named beneficiary at a time that suits them. Section 3

The five categories of items in a typical Staffordshire clearance

Every clearance breaks down into broadly the same five categories, regardless of the size of the property or the era the deceased lived through. Understanding these categories helps families prepare for the work ahead.

Family-keeper items. Photographs, letters, certain furniture pieces (often the dining table or the writing desk that everyone remembers), favourite ornaments, wedding china. These get sorted first, photographed if needed, and either set aside for collection by named family members or shipped to wherever the family wants them sent. Often 5–15% of the contents by volume.

Saleable items. Furniture in good condition, jewellery, watches, antique items, collectibles, vintage clothing, silver, ceramics with maker's marks. These go either to an auction house we work with (Hansons in Etwall, the regional Staffordshire dealers in Hanley and Burslem), to specialist dealers for specific categories, or to high-quality second-hand outlets. We provide the family with the dealer or auction valuation when received so they can make an informed decision about whether to sell. Often 15–30% of contents by value, less by volume. See more on antiques handling.

Donation items. Useable furniture, clothes in good condition, kitchenware, books, toys, gardening equipment. These go to Staffordshire charities — typically the British Heart Foundation furniture stores in Hanley and Newcastle-under-Lyme for furniture, Oxfam and Cancer Research for clothes and books, and smaller local charities for specific items. We coordinate the collection so the family doesn't need to. Often 30–50% of contents by volume.

Recyclable items. Cardboard, paper records, scrap metal, electrical items (separated under WEEE regulations), garden waste. Each goes to the appropriate Staffordshire recycling stream — paper to commercial recycling, scrap metal to local merchants in Stoke or Newcastle-under-Lyme, electrical items to the council's WEEE drop-off or charity refurbishment programmes. Often 15–25% of contents by weight.

Skip disposal. Damaged furniture beyond reasonable use, broken kitchenware, mattresses past their useful life, soiled or damp items. Final category, ideally minimised but always present in some volume. We arrange skip hire and removal, typically 1–3 skips for a typical 3-bed bereavement clearance. Often 10–20% of contents by volume.

Section 4

The realistic timeline from instruction to completion

A typical Staffordshire bereavement clearance takes 3–8 weeks from initial enquiry to completion, depending on the size of the property, the family's pace of decision-making, and the probate situation. Here's how the time typically breaks down.

Week 1: Initial enquiry and survey. The family contacts us, usually by phone, and we arrange a no-obligation site survey within 5–10 working days. The survey takes 60–90 minutes — we walk through every room with the family, discuss the family's wishes for different categories of item, identify any specifically-bequeathed items, and produce a written written quote for the clearance work.

Weeks 2–3: Family decision-making. The family takes the quote away, talks among themselves, often visits the property one or more times to physically sort the family-keeper items from the rest. We don't rush this phase — it's where the most emotionally-difficult decisions get made. Families with multiple siblings sometimes need 2–3 weeks of conversation to reach consensus on the larger items.

Weeks 3–4: First clearance visit. Once the family is ready, the crew makes the first physical visit. Family-keeper items are identified, tagged, and either collected by named family members or held at our depot for later collection. The sale-route items are identified and the auction/dealer collections are scheduled.

Weeks 4–6: Sorting and disposal. The bulk of the practical work happens here. Donation runs to Staffordshire charities, recycling centre runs, skip hire arrangement, and the auction/dealer collections. Typically 3–5 working days of crew time spread over 1–2 weeks. The family doesn't need to be present for this phase if they prefer not to be.

Weeks 6–8: Final clean and handover. The empty property gets a basic broom-clean (deeper cleaning is a separate service we can arrange), final paperwork is handed to the executor (clearance schedule, donation receipts, auction valuations), and the keys are returned. The property is ready for sale, letting, or whatever the family's chosen next step is. See our piece on move-in cleaning for the equivalent on the receiving end.

Section 5

What we keep, sell, donate, recycle, and dispose

Within the five categories above, here's a more specific breakdown of how items typically get routed during a Staffordshire bereavement clearance.

Kept items go to: named family members (most common), our depot for temporary holding while the family decides, or to a specific destination address (often the case for items bequeathed to family living overseas). We can hold items at our depot for up to 6 months at no additional charge during a clearance contract.

Saleable items go to: regional auction houses for general antiques and quality furniture (typical Staffordshire route is Hansons in Etwall, Tennants in Leyburn for higher-value items, plus the local Hanley auction rooms for general lots); jewellery dealers for jewellery and watches (typically the Hatton Garden network for higher-value pieces, local Stoke dealers for general work); specialist dealers for specific collections (model railways, vintage clothing, certain china makers, military memorabilia, vinyl records).

Donations go to: the British Heart Foundation furniture stores (Hanley, Newcastle-under-Lyme), Emmaus Stoke for general household items, Oxfam and Cancer Research for clothes and books, Donna Louise Children's Hospice and other local charity shops for specific items where they fit, and Tools for Self Reliance for tools and hardware. We provide donation receipts for the family's records.

Recyclables go to: Staffordshire County Council recycling centres (Hanley, Lichfield, Stafford), commercial paper recycling for documents and books not suitable for charity, scrap metal merchants for ferrous and non-ferrous metals (we sometimes get a small payment back from the metals which we deduct from the clearance fee), and authorised WEEE handlers for electrical items.

Disposal items go to: licensed skip hire to council-approved disposal sites. We use registered waste carriers throughout — the executor receives copies of the waste-transfer notes for the estate paperwork.

Section 6

Probate, executors, and the paperwork side

Bereavement clearances usually happen during or just after probate, and the executor (the person legally responsible for administering the estate) is our primary point of contact for the practical work. Understanding the executor's position helps the clearance run smoothly.

The executor's role includes determining and recording the value of the deceased's estate, settling any debts the estate owes, and distributing what remains according to the will. House contents are part of the estate, so any sale proceeds from clearance work technically belong to the estate and need to be recorded in the estate accounts. We provide the executor with a clearance schedule listing every significant item that was sold, donated or disposed of, with valuations where available, to support those accounts.

Granted probate before clearance. Strictly speaking, the executor shouldn't be disposing of estate assets before probate is granted. In practice, many families start the clearance work earlier — particularly the family-keeper sorting and the donation/recycling of obviously valueless items — because the property may need to be sold to fund inheritance tax payments and probate can take months. For higher-value items (anything saleable or with significant value), we recommend holding the clearance until probate is granted to avoid any complication with the estate accounting.

Inheritance Tax (IHT). If the deceased's estate exceeds the IHT threshold (£325,000 in 2026 for individuals, with various reliefs available), the executor needs to value the estate's contents accurately for the IHT return. For most ordinary household contents this is straightforward (broad market valuation), but for estates with significant antique, art or collection content the executor often commissions a formal valuation before clearance. We're happy to coordinate the formal valuation with our partner valuers in Staffordshire if needed. See our broader piece on declared values for related context.

Receipts and documentation. Throughout the clearance we provide the executor with: itemised inventory of items sold (with prices realised from auction or dealer), donation receipts from charities, waste-transfer notes from licensed disposal, and a final written summary of the clearance disposition. All this becomes part of the estate file.

Section 7

Dealing with sentimental items and family disagreements

The hardest part of a bereavement clearance is rarely the physical work — it's the emotional sorting through belongings that meant something to the deceased and may or may not mean something to the surviving family. Our approach to this part of the job is shaped by years of doing it.

We don't move sentimental items without explicit instruction. If we open a drawer and find a stack of letters, a baby's first shoes, a wedding bouquet pressed into a book, we set them aside on a clearly-marked 'family review' table and ask the executor to look at them before any decision is made. The default is to preserve until told otherwise. This adds time to the clearance but it's the right approach.

Photographs are always kept, regardless of condition. Loose photos, photo albums, photograph negatives, slide collections, home-movie reels and tapes, recent digital photo storage (USB sticks, memory cards, old phones with photos on them). We collect them in clearly-labelled boxes and hand them to the family — even photos that look unimportant often turn out to be the only image of a relative the family doesn't otherwise have a record of.

Written material. Old diaries, letters, address books, recipe books with handwritten notes, books with personal inscriptions, photo captions. Same approach as photographs — collected, labelled, handed to the family for review. Whether to keep, scan or dispose is the family's decision, not ours.

When siblings disagree over an item. We stay completely out of the disagreement. We hold the disputed item safely either at the property or at our depot, and we don't move or dispose of it until the family tells us with a single voice what to do. Family disputes over particular items can sometimes take weeks to resolve — that's fine, we wait. We've never lost an item this way and we'd rather hold something for two months than risk disposing of it before the family is settled.

Section 8

2026 costs for Staffordshire bereavement clearance

Bereavement clearance pricing reflects the size of the property, the complexity of the contents (more sortable items take longer), and any specific services the family asks for (formal valuation, holding period for items at our depot, deep cleaning of the empty property). Here are the honest 2026 ranges we quote across Staffordshire.

1-bed retirement flat or bungalow: £800–£1,400 for the full clearance. Typically 2–3 days of crew time, 1 skip, several donation and recycling runs.

2-bed retirement property or small terrace: £1,200–£2,200. Typically 3–4 days of crew time, 1–2 skips.

3-bed semi or family home: £1,800–£3,500. 4–6 days of crew time, 2–3 skips. Most typical bereavement clearance in our calendar.

4-bed detached home: £2,800–£5,200. 5–8 days of crew time, 3–4 skips. Often phased across 2–3 weeks of intermittent visits.

5+ bedroom property with substantial content: Quoted individually after survey. Typically £4,500–£10,000+ for the full clearance, often phased over 4–6 weeks.

Add-on services priced separately: formal valuation (£200–£800 depending on scale), deep cleaning of the empty property (£300–£700 for typical 3-bed), gardening/grounds clearance (£200–£500), garage and shed contents (often included in the base price unless they're substantial workshops).

Offset from item sales. Where significant items go to auction or dealer sale, the proceeds offset the clearance cost. We pass the gross sale proceeds to the estate (less the auction house's commission, which is typically 15–25%) and deduct nothing from those proceeds for the clearance fee — the clearance fee stays separate so the estate accounts are clean.

Reduced rates for charitable executors. For estates where the entire residual benefit is going to charity (no surviving family beneficiaries), we offer a 15% discount on the clearance fee. The discount applies on production of a copy of the will showing the charitable residue.

Section 9

Storage option for items the family can't decide on yet

Sometimes a family member wants to keep an item but doesn't have the space immediately, or wants to delay the decision until after probate is finalised. Our palletised storage at our Stoke-on-Trent depot is the practical answer for these cases.

Pricing is the same as our standard storage — £40–£80 per week per palletised unit — and most family-keeper items from a typical bereavement clearance fit into 1–3 units. Common use cases: a sibling living abroad who wants the family piano but won't be back to collect until next summer; an adult child setting up a first home in the next 6–12 months who'll take the dining table then; a family who hasn't decided yet whether to redistribute Grandmother's china among multiple cousins or sell it.

Stored items are accessible by appointment (typically 24-48 hours' notice), photographed at entry and re-photographed periodically to track condition. We can also coordinate later delivery to multiple destinations — for instance, the family piano going to the daughter in Newcastle-under-Lyme and the dining set going to the son in Bristol — at standard moving rates rather than the clearance rate.

For items the family is still deciding whether to keep at all, the storage period gives breathing room without the pressure of an immediate dispose-or-keep decision. Most families who use this option resolve their decisions within 3–6 months of clearance completion — beyond that, we recommend the family commits to a decision because long-term storage of low-value items rarely makes economic sense.
